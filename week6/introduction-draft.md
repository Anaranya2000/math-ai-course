# Introduction Draft

Ridge regression has long occupied a central place in statistical learning as a principled way to stabilize estimation in the presence of ill-conditioned design matrices. Since its introduction as a biased estimator for nonorthogonal problems (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970), it has become a canonical regularization method for linear models and a benchmark for understanding the bias-variance tradeoff. Yet its modern relevance extends far beyond classical least squares. In high-dimensional regimes, where the sample size is comparable to or smaller than the number of predictors, ridge regression offers a tractable route to regularization and prediction, and recent work has shown that its behavior can be characterized with striking precision in settings that were once considered analytically intractable (Cheng and Montanari, 2025). The resulting theory has connected ridge estimators to random matrix theory, equivalent sequence models, and the phenomenon of benign overfitting, revealing that ridge regression is not merely a computational workaround but a rich object of statistical theory (Wieringen, 2023; Zhang and Politis, 2021).

At the same time, the literature reveals that the classical narrative is incomplete. The standard ridge penalty shrinks coefficients toward zero, but it does not recover sparsity in the same way as the lasso; this has motivated debiasing and thresholding strategies that seek to combine the stability of ridge regression with the interpretability of sparse models (Zhang and Politis, 2021). Other lines of work have shown how stability selection can improve variable selection in challenging settings such as censored survival data (Khan, Bhadra, and Howlader, 2016), while iterative methods such as elastic gradient descent suggest that ridge-type regularization can be implemented efficiently and linked to modern optimization dynamics (Allerbo, Jonasson, and Jörnsten, 2023). These developments suggest that ridge regression is evolving from a single penalty into a modular framework for regularization, inference, and algorithm design.

Despite these advances, several open problems remain. First, current sharp characterizations of ridge risk often rely on strong covariate concentration assumptions, leaving open whether similar guarantees hold under broader or more realistic data-generating mechanisms. Second, in overparameterized settings the target parameter may be non-identifiable, raising questions about what quantity ridge regression is really estimating and how this interpretation changes with the degree of regularization. Third, some of the most powerful theoretical arguments appear to depend on assumptions that may be proof artifacts rather than statistical necessities, suggesting that the scope of the theory could be broadened by weakening these conditions. Finally, the connection between ridge-type regularization and modern overparameterized models such as neural networks remains only partially understood, particularly when regularization is combined with iterative optimization and early stopping.

This paper is motivated by the observation that ridge regression sits at an intersection of theory, computation, and modern high-dimensional inference. The method is simple enough to be widely used, yet rich enough to support deep questions about bias, variance, identifiability, and regularization. In this paper, we develop a unified framework for ridge regression that relaxes restrictive covariate assumptions, clarifies the role of non-identifiability in overparameterized regimes, and studies how debiased ridge estimators can provide both statistical efficiency and interpretable variable selection.
